    Introduction (10%)¶

    Facial emotion recognition is a significant area in the field of computer vision and artificial intelligence, focusing on identifying human emotional states from an individual’s facial expressions. Emotions such as sadness, anger, surprise, fear happiness, and neutral states are critical to recognising and understanding human communication, often conveying more than words alone and the ability to recognise these fundamental expressions is imperative in isolating the necessary features that assign a correct label. FER has many practical applications, from human-to-computer interaction, mental health monitoring, and customer service experiences.

    Literature Review (10%)¶

    FER (facial expression recognition) is fundamentally reliant on the psychological theory that expressions are universal regardless of culture. This theory was established by Ekman, P. and Friesen, W.V. (1971), who conducted pivotal research with a pre-literate culture, isolated from Western media. They demonstrated that subjects could accurately identify and replicate the same distinct expressions as understood in the Western world and provided further evidence which confirmed that six base human emotions existed being, happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust regardless of cultural exposure, which critically showed that expressions are a universe human trait. This universality is the crucial pre-requisite for computer vision, as it validates expression of emotions as, distinguishable categories that can be standardised into differing classes.

    2.1. Face Detection¶

    Before facial features can be extracted for analysis the fact must be accurately located and isolated within the image frame. The industry standard for this task is the Haar Cascades (Viola and Jones, 2001), which utilises simple rectangular features to quickly detect the presence of a face in an image. This approach ensures that the feature extraction is performed on the relevant region of interest in the images.

    2.1. Feature Extraction¶

    To accurately classify these universal emotions without deep-learning a robust appearance-based feature extraction, was created to evolve from the simple geometric approach. Early approaches to feature extraction utilised geometric tracking, which calculated the spatial distances between facial key identifiers like the corners of the mouth, or eyes (Pantic, M. and Rothkrantz, L.J.M. (2000)). The problem with the early method was a reliance on conditions, if the subject’s head pose varied or lightning conditions changed then these methods proved fragile and inaccurate. To resolve this problem further research shifted from special measurements to appearance-based methods, which analysis the texture and pixel intensity of the face as a whole, Lyons et al. (1998) demonstrated these methods were superior in this approach through the JAFFE dataset using Gabor wavelets. These are linear filters, analyse content in an image effectively replicating how the human eye detects edges.

    Within the research of appearance-based extraction one method emerged for non-deep learning applications being LBP (Local Binary Patterns), which were found to be particularly useful for analysing facial texture. Shan et al. (2009) conducted a study on low-resolution facial images, demonstrating that LBP features remain stable and robust even when face images are downsized to resolutions as low as 14x19 pixels. Their experiments established that LBP is highly effective at capturing micro-textures such as skin deformities and wrinkling, making it ideal in detecting the subtly changes in facial movements associated with emotional expression. This makes LBP particularly suitable for distinguishing emotions where the structural shape of the face changes minimally, but the surface texture shifts significantly.

    In contrast to LBP, which utilises a texture approach, the HOG approach focuses on capturing the structural shape of the face. Originally established by Dalal and Triggs (2005) for human pedestrian detection, it works by calculating the direction and intensity of gradients, effectively presenting edges within an image. By compiling these into histograms, the algorithm creates a robust representation of facial geometry such as the structural curve of the jaw or the angle of the mouth that remains stable even when lighting conditions vary. While HOG is effective for shape detection, this report prioritises LBP for its ability to capture fine even micro skin texture details despite potential deformations critical for emotional recognition.

    2.2. Classification¶

    Once the facial features are extracted it is important to select an appropriate classifier, particularly for datasets with smaller sample sizes, like JAFFE. The classifier algorithmically assigns a discrete label representing a specific emotional state to the image based on the features that were extracted. The classifier must map the image’s feature data through methods such as LBP and label it under one of the six categories of universal emotion. This process is critical for datasets with limited sample sizes, such as JAFFE where the algorithm must generalise from a small number of consistent high-quality training examples.

    For small, controlled datasets like JAFFE, one classifier stands out as an optimal choice, the SVM (Support Vector Machine). Defined by Cortes and Vapnik (1995) the algorithm operates by finding the hyperplane that maximises the margin between classes, this approach prevents the model from overfitting to noise. SVMs are inherently binary classifiers meaning that they are only able to distinguish between two classes at a time, therefore in distinguishing between multiple classes a different approach is required.

    For more complex, and high-variability datasets like Cohn-Kanade (CK), the Decision Tree classifier is an alternative to SVM. Decision Tree’s established by Quinlan (1986) is an algorithm which operates by recursively separating the data based on the feature values extracted, effectively creating decision rules to classify images. This method was established as a technique for classifying rules for higher-dimensional feature sets therefore allowing these logical rules to decompose the complex task of identifying emotions into a sequence of decisions. This approach is particularly beneficial for processing the consistent feature patterns inherent in the CK dataset. However, in comparison to the robust hyperplanes generated by SVMs, single Decision Trees can be sensitive to small variations in data, which is critical for image-based emotion recognition.

    3. Datasets (10%)¶

    Two widely publicly available datasets that were selected are the JAFFE (Japanese Female Facial Expression) and MMI Facial Expression dataset.

    The JAFFE dataset contains a cumulative total of 213 grayscale images of female Japanese students, each with a several contrasting facial expressions ranging from happiness, surprise, neutral to sadness, anger, fear, and disgust. The images which are consistent in resolution, contain appropriate file names that are clear and well labelled, for example “KL.NE2.156” which indicates the individual subject in the image KL, the expression maintained in the image NE neutral being the 2nd variation of the expression being demonstrated by the subject, the final number in the filename is 156 indicates the frame number the image was taken from. The structured naming system makes pre-processing tasks such as label extraction simple, and the uniform consistent lightning and facial positioning makes it particularly suitable for working with traditional supervised machine learning methods like SVM.

    The Cohn-Kanade (CK+48 (Shawon, A. (2018))) dataset, this project utilises a total of 927 PNG images which are sized to 48 x 48 pixels and organised by emotional state in named folders. This pre-processed format provides a consistent, high-quality data for training, which eliminates the need for frame extraction from the original CK+ which serves as a set of video sequences. One problem with this dataset is that it does not have all seven emotional categories, therefore as a resolution, the neutral folder was extracted from a smaller sample of the CK dataset and placed inside of the CK+48.

    4. Model Development (30%)¶

    4.1. Data Pre-Processing¶

    The datasets, stored in Google Drive, require minimal pre-processing to prepare the images for classification. The data is processed using Python in a Google Colab notebook, utilizing the OpenCV and OS libraries to handle image data directly from the mounted drive directory.

    4.1.1. Image Standardisation & Face Detection¶

    The first essential step in handling the images is to ensure they are consistent in lighting and isolating the regions of the face. All images are loaded from the datasets and converted into grayscale resolution; this is imperative for ensuring consistency but also to remove redundant colour information so that the LBP algorithm can prioritise based on luminance-based structure patterns, then the images are processed and resized to 48 x 48 pixels to ensure that the image data remains consistent while also retaining sufficient detail for feature extraction.

    For face detection the industry standard, Haar Cascade classifier was utilised to accurately locate and isolate the face within the image frame, once detected the image was detected the image was cropped to focus exclusively on the face region of the image, this is done to remove any irrelevant background noise on top of the conversion to Gray scaling.

    4.1.2. JAFFE Data Processing¶

    Since the JAFFE dataset consists of a single directory containing all of the images, custom file parsing was implemented to reiterate through the filenames such as KL.NE2.156.tiff to extract the emotion type, by splitting the filename to isolate the emotion type such as NE for neutral which is then mapped to its corresponding emotion label.

    4.1.3. CK+48 Data Processing¶

    The CK+48 dataset, being pre-organized by emotional state in named folders, allowed for a simpler loading process where the folder name was directly assigned as the emotion label.

    4.1.4. Data Splitting & Training¶

    To ensure both models are evaluated on completed unseen data and therefore provide a robust measure of general capability, both CK+48 and JAFFE datasets were segmented using the train_split_test function which reserves 80% of the data for training, and the remaining 20% of the data for testing, the stratify parameter was set to the target labels to ensure that training and testing maintain a proportional distribution of emotional classes from the original data, therefore preventing model biases toward majority classes.

    4.2. Feature Extraction with LBP¶

    A robust appearance-based feature extraction method was required to extract the facial expression data, for classification. The LBP (Local Binary Pattern) technique was selected over gradient-based methods like HOG for its established effectiveness in capturing micro-texture details. LBP, in research was found to be particularly useful for analysing skin deformities and wrinkling, which makes it ideal for detecting facial expression movements associated with a specific emotion. For LBP ideal parameters were chosen, these being LBP_RADIUS = 1, which is the radius to focus on micro-textures. Another parameter LBP_N_POINTS = 8, defines the number of sampled points. The LBP method uniform was chosen as to optimise the feature extraction.

    4.3. Decision Tree Classifier¶

    The decision tree is a classifier which operates by recursively separating data based on the feature values extracted effectively creating decision rules to classify images. The DecisionTreeClassifier was applied with a random_state of 42 required for reproducibility, which is essential for the consistent results required to evaluate the model.

    5. Model Evaluation¶

    The model selected for this project was Local Binary Patterns for feature extraction with Decision Trees as the classifier for the several differing emotions being sadness, anger, surprise, fear, happiness, and neutrality. Evaluation metrics were generated demonstrating different categories in how the model acted being Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and the F-1 Score.

    5.1. JAFFE Results¶


    The results for the JAFFE dataset, using the Local Binary Patterns (LBP) feature extraction and Decision Tree classifier, show significant challenges in accurately classifying emotions.

    As shown in the classification report, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 13.95%. This performance is notably low, barely exceeding random chance, being approximately 14%. The confusion matrix further highlights this difficulty, revealing a scattered distribution of predictions. The model struggled to distinguish between more nuanced emotions, for example sadness, was considerably misclassified as neutral or happy, whereas fear was often confused with sadness. This suggests that the LBP features extracted, while capturing texture, may not have been robust enough to differentiate the subtle facial shifts in the JAFFE dataset, or that the small sample size of 43 test images was insufficient for the Decision Tree to learn generalisable rules.

    Specific emotions like happy achieved a slightly higher precision, likely due to distinct features such as the position of the jaw or the visibility of the teeth, but sadness and fear returned a precision of 0.00, indicating the model failed to correctly identify a single instance of these classes. This poor performance underscores the limitations of using a single Decision Tree on a small, high-dimensional dataset without more advanced pre-processing methods.

    ## 5.2. CK+48 Dataset Results
    The CK+48 dataset, benefiting from the dataset’s larger sample size of 927 images, demonstrated an improved performance with an overall accuracy of 31.53%. While this significantly outperforms the JAFFE dataset results being 13.95%, highlighting the inherent difficulty of classifying facial expressions using simple appearance-based features like LBP with a basic Decision Tree classifier.

    As illustrated in the confusion matrix, the model showed a much stronger ability to detect distinct, high-intensity emotions compared to the JAFFE model. The Neutral emotion achieved the highest precision of 0.58, indicating that the absence of strong facial muscle deformation provided a distinct and recognisable pattern for this model.

    However, significant confusion remained among emotions that share similar facial details. Surprise was frequently misclassified as disgust by 10 instances. The Angry state suggesting the model struggled to differentiate between the open-mouth shapes of surprise and the jaw tension of anger. Happy, usually one of the easiest emotions to classify, performed poorly with a precision of just 0.18. This indicates that while the larger dataset allowed the Decision Tree to learn some general rules, the LBP features alone were insufficient to capture the subtle differences required to reliably separate complex emotions.

    # Conclusion
    This report explored the application of traditional machine learning techniques for Facial Emotion Recognition. The model consisting of Local Binary Patterns for feature extraction and a Decision Tree classifier. To investigate the model against two distinct datasets, JAFFE and CK+48 and evaluate the model’s performance and ability to generalise emotions across different datasets.

    The results demonstrated significant challenges of using single Decision Trees for image classification due to the accuracy achieved being 31.53% on the larger CK+48 dataset but performed poorly on the smaller JAFFE dataset with only 13.95% accuracy. The analysis highlighted that while LBP is effective at capturing texture details like wrinkling it lacks the structural ability to distinguish between similar but differing expressions like fear and surprise, which share similar mouth and brow deformations. Furthermore the Decision Tree classifier proved highly sensitive to noise and prone to overfitting, therefore failing to generalise between emotional states.
