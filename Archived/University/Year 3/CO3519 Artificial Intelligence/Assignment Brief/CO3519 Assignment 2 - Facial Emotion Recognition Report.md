# Introduction
Facial emotion recognition is a significant area in computer vision and artificial intelligence. It focuses on identifying six human emotions from facial expressions. Emotions being happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, and neutrality are critical in understanding how people communicate to each other. The ability to accurately recognise and isolate the distinct necessary features to deduce an appropriate emotional label to an image.

FER has many practical applications spanning human-computer interaction, mental health monitoring, driver fatigue detection, and customer service experiences. As artificial intelligence has continued to advance, automating this recognition process has become increasingly more viable and commercially significant across multiple business markets.

In the previous assignment this problem was solved using traditional features engineering methods and struggled for good performance and found some problems and challenges. Traditional machine learning approaches to FER, such as customised feature extraction methods and basic classifiers, have demonstrated considerable limitations in generalising across varied lighting conditions, differing facial orientations, and primary subject visibility. These methods have struggled to capture the proper structural depth required to truly distinguish between visually similar emotions, resulting in poor classification accuracy and limited robustness.

This project proposes the use of EfficientNet-B0, a deep convolutional neural network, to overcome these limitations by learning hierarchical facial features directly from raw image data without the dependency on manual feature engineering. The model is trained and evaluated across two datasets previously used in Assignment 1, CK+48 and JAFFE, enabling a direct performance comparison between the traditional LBP and Decision Tree approach and the proposed deep learning solution across the six fundamental facial emotions.
# Literature Review
The application of deep learning to facial emotion recognition has been extensively explored and significantly outperforms traditionally custom crafted approaches. Li and Deng (2020) conducted a comprehensive survey of deep learning methods applied to FER. The survey shows that convolutional neural networks consistently outperform traditional appearance-based methods by learning layered feature representation directly from the raw image data, removing the dependency on manual feature engineering entirely.

Khaireddin and Chen (2021) demonstrated that transfer learning applied to VGGNet achieved 73.28% accuracy on the FER-2013 dataset. This shows that pre-trained networks that are fine-tuned on emotion specific data significantly outperform models trained from scratch. This approach leverages generalised visual features learned from large-scale datasets such as ImageNet, which are then adapted to the specific task of emotion classification. However, VGG architectures are computationally expensive due to their considerable depth and parameter count, limiting their practicality in more resource constrained environments.

As a resolution to this limitation, Tan and Le (2019) proposed EfficientNet, which applies compound scaling across network width, depth, and image resolution simultaneously using a principled scaling coefficient. This approach achieves superior accuracy with substantially reduced computational cost compared to other models like VGG and ResNet. Subsequent research by Savchenko (2022) further confirmed that EfficientNet variants consistently achieve state of the art results on FER benchmarks, demonstrating strong generalisation across diverse datasets.

Another alternative is ResNet. He et al. (2016) introduced ResNet with residual connections that help train deeper networks without the degradation problem. ResNet models can achieve good performance on image classification tasks. However, like VGG, ResNet can still struggle with domain shift when applied to small, low-resolution grayscale datasets like JAFFE and CK+48.

This project utilises EfficientNet with transfer learning on the small CK+48 and JAFFE datasets. The approach is different from many previous methods because those methods often train the network from scratch on large datasets. Here transfer learning is used to adapt pre-trained ImageNet weights to the very small sample sizes and low resolution of the chosen datasets. This helps the model start with useful features instead of learning everything from the beginning with limited data.
# Datasets
The two datasets selected to train and evaluate the EfficientNet model are the CK+48 and the JAFFE dataset, both of which were utilised in Assignment 1, enabling a direct quantitative comparison between the traditional machine learning approach and the deep learning model proposed in this project.

The CK+48 dataset (Shawon, 2018) contains 927 PNG images standardised to 48×48 pixels, organised into labelled folders by emotional category. The dataset spans six emotion classes used in this project, being anger, fear, happiness, neutral, sadness, and surprise, with disgust excluded to maintain consistency with the JAFFE dataset. The pre-processed folder structure eliminates the need for frame extraction from the original CK video sequences, providing a clean and consistently formatted input for training. As identified in Assignment 1, the dataset presents challenges in distinguishing visually similar emotions such as surprise and anger due to shared facial muscle activations, which the traditional LBP and Decision Tree classifier struggled to resolve, achieving only 31.53% accuracy. Data augmentation techniques including random horizontal flipping, rotation, and zoom were applied during training to address class imbalance and improve the model’s ability to generalise across subtle inter-class variations.

The JAFFE dataset contains 213 grayscale images of ten female Japanese subjects, each demonstrating posed facial expressions across seven emotion categories, with disgust excluded to maintain label consistency with CK+48. The images are consistently lit and uniformly positioned, with structured filenames used to extract the corresponding emotion label during pre-processing, for example the filename convention “KL.NE2.156” encodes the subject identifier, the emotion code, and the frame number respectively, making automated label extraction straightforward. As demonstrated in Assignment 1, where the JAFFE model achieved only 13.95% accuracy using LBP and a Decision Tree, the dataset’s small sample size presents a considerable challenge for generalisation, making it a meaningful benchmark for evaluating the improvement offered by transfer learning.
# Model Development
Prior to classification, a preprocessing pipeline was applied to standardise all input images across both datasets. The industry standard Haar Cascade classifier (Viola and Jones, 2001) was employed for face detection, utilising a pre-trained frontal face detection model to accurately locate and isolate the facial region within each image. This approach applies a series of rectangular feature filters across the image at multiple scales, rejecting non-face regions rapidly through a cascade of increasingly complex classifiers. Although both CK+48 and JAFFE images are largely pre-cropped, applying Haar Cascade detection ensured consistent localisation of the facial region across all samples, removing irrelevant background content and aligning the region of interest before resizing. Detected face regions were subsequently resized to 224×224 pixels to meet EfficientNet’s input requirements, with pixel values normalised to the range [0, 1] to stabilise gradient updates during training.

EfficientNet-B0 was selected as the primary architecture for this project, leveraging transfer learning to adapt a model pre-trained on ImageNet, a large-scale dataset containing 1.4 million labelled images across 1,000 categories. Through this pre-training, EfficientNet develops a robust understanding of low-level visual features such as edges, textures, and shapes, which are directly transferable to the task of facial emotion recognition. Rather than initialising the model with random weights and training from scratch, transfer learning retains these pre-learned representations and replaces only the final classification layer with a new dense layer outputting six emotion categories. This approach significantly reduces the volume of labelled data required to achieve strong performance, which is particularly critical given the limited sample size of the JAFFE dataset. The pre-trained weights therefore provide a strong initialisation point, allowing the model to converge faster and generalise more effectively across both datasets than any traditional machine learning approach could achieve.

The model was trained using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) as the optimiser, updating the model’s weights iteratively by computing the loss across small batches of training images. A learning rate of 0.001 was selected to control the magnitude of each weight update, sufficiently conservative to allow stable convergence without overshooting the optimal solution. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) was chosen as the optimiser instead of Adam. SGD was preferred because it tends to be more stable when working with small datasets like CK+48 and JAFFE. Adam can sometimes converge too quickly and overfit on limited data, while SGD with a small learning rate updates the weights more gradually and helps the model generalise better in this case. Categorical cross-entropy was used as the loss function, measuring the divergence between the predicted probability distribution across the six emotion classes and the true label, penalising confident incorrect predictions more heavily than uncertain ones. The model was trained for up to 30 epochs with a batch size of 32, meaning 32 images were processed per weight update, balancing training stability with computational efficiency. To reduce overfitting, dropout regularisation was applied prior to the final classification layer at a rate of 0.5, randomly deactivating half of the neurons during each training step to prevent the model from becoming overly dependent on any single feature pathway. An early stopping callback monitored validation loss throughout training and halted the process if no improvement was observed after five consecutive epochs, restoring the best performing weights automatically. A learning rate reduction callback was additionally applied, halving the learning rate after three epochs of stagnating validation loss to allow finer convergence near the optimal solution.
# Model Evaluation
The performance of the fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0 model was evaluated on the held-out test sets of the CK+48 and JAFFE datasets. The analysis of the training logs reveals significant challenges in convergence and generalisation for this specific application.

On the CK+48 dataset, the model achieved a final test accuracy of 24%. The classification report indicates a severe class imbalance in the model's predictive capability. Precision and recall scores for the classes "angry", "fear", "neutral", and "sad" were recorded at 0.00, meaning the model failed to correctly identify a single instance of these four emotional states. The model only produced non-zero predictions for the "happy" and "surprise" classes. While the recall for "happy" was 0.60, the corresponding precision was low at 0.22, indicating that a large number of non-happy images were incorrectly labelled as happy.

# Conclusion
This project implemented a deep learning approach to facial emotion recognition using the EfficientNet-B0 architecture with transfer learning from ImageNet weights. The model was trained and evaluated on two publicly available datasets, CK+48 and JAFFE, with the objective of classifying six fundamental emotions: anger, fear, happiness, neutral, sadness, and surprise. A preprocessing pipeline incorporating Haar Cascade face detection, image resizing to 224×224 pixels, and pixel normalisation was employed to standardise input data. Training utilised Stochastic Gradient Descent with a learning rate of 0.001, categorical cross-entropy loss, and regularisation techniques including dropout at a rate of 0.5, early stopping, and learning rate reduction on plateau.

The results obtained from model evaluation highlight significant limitations in applying a large pre-trained architecture to low-resolution facial expression data. On the CK+48 dataset, the model achieved a test accuracy of 24%, with zero predictive capability for the anger, fear, neutral, and sadness classes. The model predominantly predicted the happy and surprise categories, demonstrating a tendency to collapse predictions into a narrow subset of available labels. Analysis of the training curves indicated that both training and validation loss plateaued early in the training process, with minimal improvement observed despite reductions in learning rate. This underfitting suggests that the model was unable to extract sufficiently discriminative features from the 48×48 pixel inputs after upscaling, and that the transfer learning benefit was diminished by the resolution gap between ImageNet and the target datasets.

Performance on the JAFFE dataset was even more problematic. Although the test accuracy was recorded at 20%, inspection of the confusion matrix and sample predictions revealed a complete failure of the classification mechanism. The model assigned the label sad to every single test image, regardless of the true expression. This behaviour indicates that the model converged to a degenerate solution where the final dense layer weights were biased entirely toward a single output class. Given the extremely limited training set of 147 images, the model lacked sufficient exposure to diverse facial configurations to learn meaningful decision boundaries, and the class weighting applied during training was insufficient to counteract this collapse.

Several future directions could be pursued to address the shortcomings identified in this work. First, a custom convolutional neural network designed specifically for low-resolution inputs of 48×48 pixels would likely preserve critical texture information that is lost during upscaling to 224×224 pixels. Second, more aggressive data augmentation strategies such as random erasing or CutMix could artificially expand the effective size of the CK+48 dataset and improve generalisation. Third, for the JAFFE dataset, few-shot learning techniques would be more appropriate than standard transfer learning, as they are explicitly designed to operate with very limited labelled examples. Fourth, employing a larger and more diverse emotion dataset such as FER2013 or AffectNet would provide a stronger foundation for fine-tuning a model of EfficientNet's capacity.

Through the completion of this project, the selection of a deep learning architecture must be carefully matched to the characteristics of the available data. While EfficientNet is a state-of-the-art model for large-scale image classification, its application to small, low-resolution facial expression datasets proved ineffective in this instance. The experience underscored the importance of critically evaluating training dynamics, such as loss curves and confusion matrices, rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy metrics. Furthermore, the project reinforced the understanding that transfer learning is not a universal solution and that domain-specific adaptations are essential for achieving robust performance in specialised computer vision tasks.
